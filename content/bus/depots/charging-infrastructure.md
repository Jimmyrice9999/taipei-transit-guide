Taiwan publishes no consolidated national registry of bus depots and their
chargers. This page is deliberately an overview built from named
installations and city/national figures, not a census — see
[bus depots and operating sites](/bus/depots/) for the per-depot pages this
site does have.

## National public-charger figures mostly aren't about buses

MOTC reports 12,334 public charging connectors nationally as of end-July
2025 — but the figure is explicitly measured against 110,768 registered
electric **passenger cars** (電動小客車), giving a 9.0:1 ratio that says
nothing about buses[^motc-chargers]. A December 2025 report gives 13,482
connectors and an 8.8:1 overall ratio attributed to MOTC, but states no
vehicle category at all[^cnyes-chargers] — it very plausibly continues the
same largely-passenger-car series, but no source confirms this either
way, and the figure should not be read as bus-specific charging capacity.
The Forward-Looking Infrastructure Program's own NT$9.8 billion
public-charger initiative states directly that its demand-assessment tool
"specifically targets electric sedans," not buses[^iot-charger-tool]. The
programme's own July 2025 figure, from the same MOTC release quoted
above, gives 4,768 chargers approved and 1,653 activated by that
date[^motc-chargers] — a lower approved count than an unverified,
unfetched search-summary figure of 4,865 approved by December 2024, an
unreconciled and unsourced drop this page does not resolve.

## No dedicated depot-electrification budget line found

The 2030 electric-bus programme's FY2025 budget, verified from a
Legislative Yuan primary document, has four lines: vehicle-purchase
subsidy (NT$5.92bn), operating subsidy (NT$797.2m), route-network
optimisation subsidy (NT$130.8m) and maintenance-system support
(NT$9.5m)[^ly-doc-c]. **None is labelled as charging-infrastructure or
depot-electrification spending.** Route-network optimisation is the
closest candidate but is not confirmed to cover charging installations.

## Named installations

**Beitou depot (北投機廠), Taipei** — Taipei's electric buses gained a
shared night-charging facility inside TRTC's Beitou MRT depot on 2 July
2025: 11 charging posts, 22 bays, run by Danan Bus but open to any
operator, coordinated with TRTC by the city's transport bureau[^nextapple-beitou].
The rationale given is a genuinely elegant piece of infrastructure
sharing: the metro draws its power mainly by day, electric buses charge
mainly by night, so pairing the two uses the same land and grid capacity
for both[^nextapple-beitou].

**南港站 (Nangang), Capital Bus** — 8 charging posts, 16 guns, with 30
buses rotating through them and each charge taking 3–4 hours[^twreporter-charging].

**二重站, New Taipei** — roughly 38 buses sharing only 5 charging bays,
requiring a charge after nearly every run[^twreporter-charging]. Which
operator runs this depot is not stated by the source found.

**木柵二站 (Muzha #2), Xinxin Bus** — Taipei's first electric-bus
fast-charging station, opened February 2023, reaching 80% charge in 20
minutes on a CCS1 connector — "matching what most of Taiwan's electric
buses currently use" — and explicitly built as a shared facility under
central policy, likened by its own coverage to a petrol-station
model[^udn-muzha].

## Cost and delay, from named industry figures

A charger for a large electric bus costs roughly NT$3 million, "not
counting the cost of the Taipower feed-in application process," per a
named Taiwan Motor Transport manager[^twreporter-charging]. Converting a
depot — removing fuel tanks, applying for a Taipower feed, installing
transformers and chargers — typically takes one to two years of
administrative process and tens of millions of NT dollars, per a named
Capital Bus deputy manager[^twreporter-charging]. Taipower feed-in
applications can sit unapproved for months, a real operational
difficulty[^twreporter-charging].

## Charging standard: CCS1

An ITRI-led alliance of roughly 50 companies selected CCS1 as Taiwan's
unified public fast-charging standard in March 2021, reasoning that
Taiwan's electrical grid environment resembles the United States' and
that CCS1 is compatible with the existing J1772 slow-charge
base[^bnext-ccs1]. That announcement addressed electric vehicles
generally, not buses specifically — but bus-specific use is independently
confirmed: Tron Energy's current [RAC-700](/bus/models/rac-700/) bus
charges via CCS1 with dual guns, and the Muzha depot above independently
uses CCS1[^udn-muzha].

## A city-level combined figure: Keelung

Keelung plans to replace its remaining 164 diesel city buses and build 8
charging stations by 2026, a combined NT$1.8 billion — an audit found the
programme running significantly behind schedule[^ltn-keelung]. The source
does not split the NT$1.8 billion between vehicles and charging
infrastructure.
