The orange line is the network's only Y: one trunk south to Zhonghe, and two
arms north-west that share it — one to Luzhou, one through Xinzhuang to
Huilong[^dorts-luzhou]. It was not built as one railway. It is three separate projects,
approved and contracted years apart, that TRTC joined into a single operated
line only in 2012[^dorts-zhonghe][^dorts-xinzhuang]. The awkward compound
name is the residue of that history, and so is one of the network's stranger
details: the line's own station numbers jump from **O21** straight to
**O50**[^zh-zhonghe-xinlu].

![An orange-liveried train at the platform of Luzhou Station, its name sign overhead](/images/zhonghe-xinlu-line/station-800.webp "Luzhou Station, on the line's northern Luzhou arm. — 李元顥, public domain, Wikimedia Commons")

## Three projects, one line

DORTS still documents the line as three separate route descriptions, and
reading them against each other is what actually explains the geography.
The **Zhonghe line 中和線** runs north from Nanshijiao and ends at the
Roosevelt Road/Heping East Road junction, "連接" to Guting station on the
Xindian line[^dorts-zhonghe]. The **Xinzhuang line 新莊線** begins *at*
Guting and runs north through Sanchong to Xinzhuang[^dorts-xinzhuang]. Each
project's own text stops where the other's starts: **Guting is the boundary
between them**, confirmed independently from both sides rather than asserted
by a single source.

North of the trunk, the **Luzhou line 蘆洲線** peels off. Its own route
description places the fork precisely: it begins at "the Xinzhuang line's
track switch, northwest of Taipei Bridge"[^dorts-luzhou] — that is, between
**O12** Daqiaotou and **O13** Taipei Bridge, one stop north of the junction
station most maps imply. Daqiaotou is still the last stop every train shares,
which is why the platform signage there matters more than anywhere else on
the network — but the physical track divide is a stop further on[^dorts-luzhou].

Each project kept its own length and station count: Zhonghe **5.4 km, 4
stations**; Xinzhuang **19.7 km, 16 stations**; Luzhou **6.4 km, 5
stations**[^dorts-zhonghe][^dorts-xinzhuang][^dorts-luzhou]. Add them and the
stations come to 25, one short of the line's published total of
26[^dorts-zhonghe][^dorts-xinzhuang][^dorts-luzhou]. The
missing station is Guting itself: DORTS's own Zhonghe-line page lists it but
does not count it, noting **"本站工程併入捷運新店線辦理"** — its construction
was carried out as part of the Xindian line project, not
Zhonghe's[^dorts-zhonghe]. Add that one station back and the totals
reconcile: 4 + 1 + 16 + 5 = 26[^dorts-zhonghe][^dorts-xinzhuang][^dorts-luzhou].

That same three-project history is why the numbering breaks in the middle.
Xinzhuang's stations run **O01–O21**; Luzhou's occupy a separate block,
**O50–O54**, rather than continuing the sequence. Nothing found in this
research states DORTS's reasoning for choosing the O50–O54 block specifically
rather than simply continuing past O21 — it is recorded here as an observed
fact of the numbering, not an explained one.

## Opening, section by section

| Section | Opened |
| --- | --- |
| Zhonghe line, Guting–Nanshijiao | 24 December 1998[^dorts-zhonghe] |
| Xinzhuang line, Taipei section, Daqiaotou–Zhongxiao Xinsheng | 3 November 2010[^dorts-xinzhuang] |
| Luzhou line, Luzhou–Daqiaotou | 3 November 2010[^dorts-luzhou] |
| Xinzhuang line, New Taipei section, Daqiaotou–Fu Jen University | 5 January 2012[^dorts-xinzhuang] |
| Dongmen (O06), Zhongxiao Xinsheng–Guting | 30 September 2012[^dorts-xinzhuang] |
| Fu Jen University–Huilong | 29 June 2013[^dorts-xinzhuang] |

The Luzhou line's opening date is worth stating plainly because it is not
inferred from the Xinzhuang line's schedule: DORTS's own Luzhou-line page
gives the identical inspection and opening dates (4 September, 16 October,
3 November 2010) in its own right[^dorts-luzhou].

**Dongmen is the connector that made this one line rather than two.** Before
30 September 2012, the Zhonghe section (which had run through with the
Xindian and Danshui lines since 1998) and the Xinlu section (Xinzhuang plus
Luzhou, opened from 2010) were operationally separate railways that happened
to nearly touch at Guting[^dorts-xinzhuang][^zh-zhonghe-xinlu]. Dongmen's opening closed that half-kilometre gap,
and TRTC's service pattern shifted to the branch-to-branch trains that run
today[^dorts-xinzhuang][^zh-zhonghe-xinlu]. The whole route was assigned
**"4號線"** on 11 October 2014 and its station codes changed from numbers
alone to the current **O**-prefixed system in October 2016[^zh-zhonghe-xinlu].

## How the branches run

Every train starts or ends at **O01 Nanshijiao**, in Zhonghe[^dorts-zhonghe]. From there it
either continues north-west through Xinzhuang to **O21 Huilong**, or forks at
the Taipei Bridge junction to **O54 Luzhou**[^dorts-luzhou]. TRTC's own
service page states the pattern directly, as **"迴龍－南勢角"** and
**"蘆洲－南勢角"**, and gives one-way running times of about **48 minutes**
for the Huilong run and **38 minutes** for the shorter Luzhou
run[^trtc-headways] — figures independently matched, to the minute, by the
TravelTime field on the two branch routes in Taiwan's TDX open-data
platform[^tdx]. zh.wikipedia additionally labels the branches **4A**
(towards Xinzhuang/Huilong) and **4B** (towards Luzhou)[^zh-zhonghe-xinlu],
though TRTC's own current service page does not use those letters in the
text this research read.

Headways compound on the shared trunk. TRTC publishes about **6
minutes**[^trtc-headways] per branch at weekday peak, which becomes about
**3 minutes** where both overlap between Nanshijiao and Daqiaotou; about
**9–10 minutes** per branch off-peak (**4.5–5 minutes** shared); and about
**12 minutes** per branch after 23:00 (**6 minutes** shared). Weekends and
holidays run the off-peak pattern all day from 06:00[^trtc-headways].

## Budget and contracts

The Xinzhuang and Luzhou lines were planned and financed as one combined
project. The Executive Yuan approved the route plan on **17 September
1994**, the EPA accepted the environmental impact statement on **28 January
1995**, and the finance plan was approved on **15 July 1998** at an original
total budget of **NT$167.69 billion**[^dorts-xinzhuang]. A fifth revised plan,
approved by the Executive Yuan on **10 June 2019** — six years after the
last section opened — set the total at **NT$161.19 billion**, lower than the
original figure[^dorts-xinzhuang]. DORTS's own page does not itemise the
second through fourth revisions and states no reason for the decrease; **why
a Taiwanese metro project's budget fell rather than rose is TBC**, and would
be settled by an itemised revision history or an audit report, neither found
in this research.

Construction was split into section contracts by city: four in Taipei
(**CK570A, CK570B, CK570G, CK570H**) and five in New Taipei (**CK570C,
CK570D, CK570E, CK570F, CK570J**), covering civil works, water and
environmental control, lifts and escalators, and finishes. Electrical and
mechanical systems and rolling stock were procured jointly with the Nangang
line's eastern extension and other TRTC stock purchases[^dorts-xinzhuang].
The earliest contract of any kind was **IKTX02**, a temporary access road
over the Erchong floodway levee, signed 7 February 2001; the earliest main
civil-works contract was **CK570D**, signed 29 June 2001[^dorts-xinzhuang].

Trains were bought by the same three-project split: DORTS's own procurement
record gives the Zhonghe line's CC361 contract as 8 six-car trains, the
Xinzhuang line's CK371 as 20, and the Luzhou line's CL601 as 4 — 32 trains as
originally ordered[^dorts-fleet-faq]. All three ran as
[C371](/rail/metro/rolling-stock/c371/) stock; the fleet's current in-service total
is not established on this page.

## Zhonghe Depot — built to fit, not to spare

**Zhonghe Depot 中和機廠** — which has no page of its own on this site yet —
sits on the former Taiwan
Railways Zhonghe station site, south of Heping Street, and includes
Nanshijiao station itself within its stated **1.47-hectare**
footprint[^dorts-zhonghe]. DORTS gives its own reason for the depot's narrow
job list — stabling, general maintenance and cleaning, nothing
more — in one clause: **"因用地面積之限制"**, because the site is too small
for anything else[^dorts-zhonghe]. zh.wikipedia separately gives a smaller
area, about 1.1 hectares, and describes the depot as underground with six
stabling tracks — neither of which DORTS's own page states, and neither
reconciled here[^zh-zhonghe-depot].

## Xinzhuang Depot and the Losheng Sanatorium

[Xinzhuang Depot](/rail/metro/depots/xinzhuang-depot/), beside Huilong station and
straddling the New Taipei–Taoyuan boundary, stands on part of the former
grounds of **Losheng Sanatorium 樂生療養院**, established in 1930 under
Japanese rule as Taiwan's first and only public leprosarium[^moc-losheng]. DORTS's own
account of the depot states plainly that its layout was changed for the
preservation dispute: **"新莊機廠因配合樂生療養院保存案計畫，調整機廠設施的配置"**[^dorts-xinzhuang] — the operator's own confirmation that a
patients'-rights campaign altered a piece of civil engineering, not a
claim made only by the campaign itself.

The dispute ran for years and involved real people displaced twice: once by
the state's mid-century policy of compulsory isolation for Hansen's disease,
and again by the depot that was built on the hospital's land. The Youth
Losheng Alliance (青年樂生聯盟) formed among medical students in 2004, and
residents themselves formed the Losheng Preservation Self-Rescue Association
(樂生保留自救會) in 2005[^e-info-losheng]. In March 2007 a one-month
demolition notice produced clashes with police; preservation supporters
crowdfunded roughly NT$200,000 in 24 hours to run a newspaper advertisement
comparing preservation options[^e-info-losheng]. On 11 April 2007 Premier
蘇貞昌 apologised in person and pledged **"保留90%為最高目標"** — 90%
preservation as the highest goal — and thousands marched days
later[^e-info-losheng].

What was actually decided did not reach that pledge. The Ministry of
Culture's own record of the resulting **"530 plan"** (30 May 2007) states
**39 buildings preserved in place, 6 demolished, and 9 relocated or
rebuilt**[^moc-losheng] — a compromise between an earlier 41.6% plan from
the county government and a 90%, 46-building plan evaluated for the Council
for Cultural Affairs[^moc-losheng]. Independent reporting found that even
this compromise was not delivered in full: 焦點事件 reports that the Youth
Losheng Alliance discovered the plan, once carried out, had actually
preserved only **28 buildings** — in their own words, little more than a
repackaging of the 41.6% plan it was meant to
improve on[^eventsinfocus-losheng]. These figures — 39, "40 preserved" as
焦點事件 separately characterises the same decision, and the campaigners'
count of 28 actually delivered — do not agree with each other, and none is
resolved here in the others' favour[^moc-losheng][^eventsinfocus-losheng].

The construction contract itself was disrupted by the same dispute. DORTS's
Xinzhuang-line page does not give a date for when the original **CK570F**
depot contract was awarded. A campaign-organisation account
(台灣人權促進會) gives two dates on one page — a contract signing on 21 May
2002 and, separately, a tender won on 19 April 2004 — in an order that
cannot both describe the same award[^tahr-602]. What is independently
documented is the contract's end: its original contractor, 工信工程,
terminated it on 2 January 2008 after 183 days' suspension over the Losheng
case, and the work was re-tendered as **CK570J** in June 2008, won by
大陸工程, which broke ground on 26 November 2008[^zh-xinzhuang-depot]. The
original award date stays **TBC**; a Government e-Procurement record or a
contemporaneous 2002–2004 news report naming the contract would settle it.

## Luzhou Depot

[Luzhou Depot](/rail/metro/depots/luzhou-depot/) sits at the end of the Luzhou arm,
bounded by the Tamsui River levee to the north, the Shuinangou drainage
channel to the south, the Luzhou pumping station to the west and a planned
road to the east — a site about 800 m long and, by DORTS's own figure, up to
**280 m** wide, covering **16.8 hectares**[^dorts-luzhou]. Its published
facilities run to a train wash, entry and exit lines, a maintenance workshop,
a separate civil/track workshop, a test track, outdoor stabling tracks, a
storage yard, the main substation, and an administration and training
building[^dorts-luzhou]. zh.wikipedia gives a conflicting widest-point figure
of 300 m, and separately describes the depot's grade as having been raised
from Level 3 to a "quasi-Level-5" facility specifically to absorb
maintenance work that Xinzhuang Depot could no longer carry once its own
footprint was cut back for the Losheng preservation plan — one depot's
constraint becoming another depot's added workload, if that account is
right[^zh-luzhou-depot].

## How long the line is

Three figures, and they are not really in conflict once labelled. **31.5 km
and 26 stations** is the sum of the three component projects' own DORTS
figures (5.4 + 19.7 + 6.4, with the station count reconciled above), and it
matches the total zh.wikipedia's infobox gives
outright[^dorts-zhonghe][^dorts-xinzhuang][^dorts-luzhou][^zh-zhonghe-xinlu].
That is the whole network as built. TDX instead measures each branch as it
actually runs from Nanshijiao: **24.11 km** to Huilong, **18.41 km** to
Luzhou[^tdx] — figures a passenger's own journey matches, but which overlap
each other along the shared trunk and so cannot simply be added to the
network total.
