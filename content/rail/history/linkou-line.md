## One corridor, several milestones

The Linkou Line, 林口線, is not safely described by one opening date. Taiwan
Railways' construction-history table gives it a 19.2 km length, construction in
1966 and operation started in 1968.[^tra-construction-history] The operator's
chronology separately records a passenger-train opening ceremony on 27 October
2005.[^tra-timeline] Those entries are both official, but they describe
different phases: the first is the construction-history table's operation-start
field, while the second is a dated passenger-service ceremony.[^tra-construction-history][^tra-timeline]

This distinction matters because the line's public life is otherwise easy to
compress into a false “opened in 2005, closed in 2013” story.[^tra-construction-history][^tra-timeline][^motc-101-yearbook] The sources support
that passenger-era shorthand only if the earlier 1968 value is hidden.[^tra-construction-history][^tra-timeline] This page
keeps the values side by side instead.[^tra-construction-history][^tra-timeline]

## Closure and the 2012–13 boundary

The closure also has two official dates. The construction-history table labels
the Linkou Line as stopped operating in ROC year 102, or 2013.[^tra-construction-history]
MOTC's 101 yearbook records a more specific operational milestone: on 31
December 2012, the line's suspension was accompanied by all 45 level crossings
being taken out of use.[^motc-101-yearbook] The yearbook's date and the
construction table's year are not collapsed into one invented legal-closure
date. They are retained as a boundary between a dated shutdown action and the
year label used by the later construction-history table.[^motc-101-yearbook][^tra-construction-history]

The current TDX TRA snapshot contains no live Linkou route record. That is a
data-boundary statement about the checked current dataset, not a claim that the
former alignment has disappeared physically.[^tra-construction-history]

## What happened to the corridor

The Railway Bureau later described the former corridor in a planning document
as approximately 19.2 km long.[^rb-linkou-corridor] It says the project was
originally a “Taiwan Railways Linkou Line corridor revitalisation” project and
was renamed after Taoyuan City applied for funding under the central Town Heart
programme.[^rb-linkou-corridor] That is planning history, not evidence of a
reopened railway: the document discusses corridor and surrounding-space
improvement, not a current passenger timetable or a new operating route.[^rb-linkou-corridor]

The repeated 19.2 km value should be read with the same caution.[^tra-construction-history][^rb-linkou-corridor] The operator's
construction table gives a line distance, while the later bureau document gives
the scope of a corridor project.[^tra-construction-history][^rb-linkou-corridor]
The sources do not establish that every metre of the former track remained in
place or that both documents measured the same surviving infrastructure.

## Remaining records

The fetched primary records do not provide one complete, citable historical
station list, a dated legal closure gazette, a full track-removal chronology or
a segment-level historical gauge record.[^tra-construction-history][^motc-101-yearbook][^rb-linkou-corridor]
They also do not settle which former structures survive along the corridor. Those
fields remain TBC until TRA, the Railway Bureau or an archival source publishes
the relevant register.[^tra-construction-history][^rb-linkou-corridor]
