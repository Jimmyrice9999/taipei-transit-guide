## Six transport projects under one 1970s banner

Premier Chiang Ching-kuo announced the Ten Major Construction Projects
(十大建設) in 1973, to be completed 1974-1979, in response to the 1973 oil
crisis and infrastructure bottlenecks from a decade of rapid
industrialisation.[^zhwiki-ten-projects] Six of the ten were transport
projects: the Sun Yat-sen Freeway (Keelung to Kaohsiung, 373 km, completed
31 October 1978), railway electrification of the western trunk line
(completed 27 June 1979), the North-Link Line connecting the eastern rail
network to the western one (full operation from 1 February 1980), Taoyuan
International Airport (opened 26 February 1979 as Chiang Kai-shek
International Airport), Taichung Port, and Su'ao Port.[^zhwiki-ten-projects]
The freeway's construction cost is reported with three different figures
across the same secondary source — NT$42.9 billion, "over NT$40 billion,"
and NT$44.8 billion — none of which this research traced to a primary
government figure, so all three are published here rather than
resolved.[^zhwiki-freeway] A separate full-page fetch of a popular history
site, checked specifically because a search-engine summary had implied it
documented a substantive motorway-versus-rail policy debate, found only a
much thinner account: that Chiang overrode unspecified objections in 1970,
with no named opponents and no cost comparison — an example of a search
snippet outrunning what the underlying page actually
supports.[^storystudio-freeway] No source found in this research documents
a contemporaneous, sourced debate over widening the existing rail/road
corridor as an alternative to a new freeway alignment.

## A different model, 25 years later: THSR as BOT

Taiwan High Speed Rail was procured under a fundamentally different model:
Build-Operate-Transfer, enabled by the 獎勵民間參與交通建設條例 (Statute for
Encouraging Private Participation in Transportation Construction),
promulgated in December 1994.[^ly-thsr-history] The Ministry of
Transportation and Communications and Taiwan High Speed Rail Corporation
signed the 35-year construction-operation contract and a separate 50-year
station-area development contract on 23 July 1998.[^ly-thsr-history] The
Legislative Yuan's own account of this history documents the legal and
contractual mechanics in detail but does not itself explain why BOT was
chosen over conventional government construction — that policy rationale
appears only in secondary sources this research could not verify against a
primary Executive Yuan or MOTC record, and is not asserted here as
established.[^ly-thsr-history]

## What the Control Yuan called a "knockoff BOT"

Operating revenue fell short of projections after the March 2007 opening,
and by 2009 THSR faced a funding gap for principal and interest
repayment.[^ly-thsr-history] MOTC coordinated an interest-rate reduction on
roughly NT$391 billion of loans in December 2008, from 8% down to
2.7%-4%.[^ly-thsr-history] A Control Yuan investigation report, dated 9
December 2009, examined the BOT structure directly and found that THSR's
execution risk had, in substance, been transferred entirely onto the
government — "由全民買單" (footed by the whole citizenry) — and gave the
scale of that transfer in figures: government-arranged bank financing of
NT$223.4 billion, 80.07% of the entire domestic banking-consortium loan
pool; a total government financing guarantee of NT$308.3 billion (NT$323.3
billion including a performance bond); and direct government/state-
enterprise equity of NT$39.4 billion, a 37.4% stake.[^cy-thsr-bot-report]
Against this, the original private consortium had pledged NT$52.9 billion
in capital (51% of the total) but had actually paid in only NT$29.46
billion (27.9%) by June 2008 — a shortfall against its own pledge that
triggered no contractual penalty, per the same
report.[^cy-thsr-bot-report] The report's own characterisation is explicit:
it calls Taiwan's version a "山寨版BOT" (a knockoff BOT) departing from the
textbook principle, contrasting it with the Channel Tunnel project, where
the British and French governments held to "no investment, no guarantee, no
clean-up."[^cy-thsr-bot-report]

The financial crisis brought a change in company leadership — chairman 殷琪,
representing the original private capital, was replaced by 歐晉德,
representing public shareholders — and a January 2010 debt restructuring
that also changed the depreciation method from straight-line to a
usage-based calculation, reducing the reported loss in THSR's early
years.[^ly-thsr-history][^gvm-thsr-crisis] These later-crisis details rest
on a secondary synthesis this research did not independently re-verify
against THSR's own financial reports or a further Control Yuan or Audit
Office document, and are reported with that caveat rather than as
independently confirmed.[^gvm-thsr-crisis]

## Records still missing

The Sun Yat-sen Freeway's actual construction cost is not established by
any primary government source located here; three conflicting secondary
figures exist instead. A genuine, contemporaneous motorway-versus-rail
policy debate is not established by any source found — only a thin,
unspecific "overrode objections" account. The explicit policy rationale for
choosing BOT over government self-construction for THSR is likewise not
established — the Legislative Yuan's own primary account documents the
mechanics but not the reasoning. A claimed July 1993 legislative vote
deleting the original government-construction budget is unverified; it was
found only in unattributed sources during this research and is not
published here as established.
