Taiwan's first metro line was bought as a French turnkey system in 1988, caught
fire twice on the test track in 1993, opened in 1996 more than four years
late, and lost its builder's maintenance staff not long afterwards[^zh-muzha].
Seventeen years after the signing, the Supreme Court ordered Taipei to pay
Matra roughly **NT$1.64 billion**, and the city paid[^ltn-2005].

Almost all tellings run this together into one story about a French company
storming off. It is two stories, thirteen years apart, and telling them apart
is the point of this page.

## Two disputes, not one

**The delay claim, 1988–2005.** Matra sued for the cost of a contract that ran
years longer than it was written for, because the civil works its equipment
depended on were late — the claim arises, in 自由時報's words,
「因土木工程部分包商施工延誤，連帶造成馬特拉的工期延長，增加成本費用」[^ltn-2005].
This is the dispute that went to arbitration in 1993 and was still before the
Supreme Court in 2005[^ltn-2005]. It is about money and contract law, and
**Matra won it**[^ltn-2005].

**The withdrawal, 1996–97.** After the line opened, Matra pulled its technical
staff out of Taiwan and stopped maintaining the system[^zh-wenhu]. This is the
dispute everybody remembers — the one that produced the most famous sentence
said about the Taipei Metro, and the one that steered the procurement that
followed.

They are connected: the withdrawal happened because the delay claim had
poisoned the relationship. But they are not the same event, and the
NT$1.64 billion that circulates belongs to the first, not the
second[^ltn-2005].

## The contract

| | | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matra selected as preferred bidder | 20 April 1988 | [^zh-muzha] |
| Negotiations concluded | 25 June 1988 | [^zh-muzha] |
| Contract signed | 13 July 1988 | [^zh-muzha][^ltn-2005] |
| Value | ≈ NT$7.87 billion | [^zh-muzha] |
| Scope | Muzha Line E&M, design, 51 two-car sets, testing, training | [^zh-muzha] |

The scope matters more than the number. Taipei did not hire a contractor to
electrify a railway; it bought a complete driverless system — vehicles,
control, design and testing in one contract[^ltn-2005]. That single fact
decides both halves of this story: it is why the courts eventually read the
deal the way they did, and it is why losing the vendor meant losing the means
to run the railway.

黃令名's account of the tender is that it was deliberately written to a
「歐美標準」 — a European-or-American standard rather than a US-only one —
which opened it to European bidders, and that VAL was then the most mature
automated system on the market[^huang-2021]. He also records, without
endorsing, that the award fed rumours connecting it to arms purchases from
France[^huang-2021]. He reports them as rumours; so does this page.

## The fires, and the engineering behind them

There is an actual mechanism here, and it is better than "the French trains
kept catching fire".

The trains were designed and run in France as **two-car units**. For Taipei
the capacity specification became **four cars**, and the automatic train
control was not redesigned to match — two two-car units were coupled and run
on a shared signal path[^huang-2021][^ltn-2008]. When the link between them
failed, the units disagreed about what to do next: in 黃令名's reconstruction
the lead unit began to move while the trailing unit, having heard nothing,
kept its brakes locked[^huang-2021]; in 自由時報's, braking seized one axle
pair while another drove on —
「煞車咬死，輪胎空轉摩擦過熱起火燃燒」[^ltn-2008].

The two accounts differ in that detail and agree in everything that matters:
a capacity change outran the signalling design, brakes dragged, and on rubber
tyres against a concrete running surface, dragging makes heat and heat makes
fire[^huang-2021][^ltn-2008]. On a steel-wheel railway the same failure
produces wheel flats and noise. The fires were the tyres' version.

Two independent tellings, three years apart, is decent corroboration for a
causal chain — but neither is an accident investigation, and none has been
found. That gap is flagged again at the foot of this page.

Most of this chronology is one encyclopedia entry deep[^zh-muzha]. The two
fires themselves are better anchored: 自由時報 places them in May and
September of 1993[^ltn-2008], and its 2005 commentary invokes 「接連被兩把大火
燒得焦黑的電聯車，以及將近兩百根暴露著裂縫的捷運支柱」 — two burned trains and
nearly two hundred cracked columns[^ltn-2005].

Those cracked columns deserve their own sentence. Cap beams are civil works,
not Matra's electrical and mechanical scope — they belong to the other
contractors on the project[^ltn-2005]. The same programme that burned Matra's
trains in public was also, more quietly, running late underneath them, and
that lateness is what Matra's claim was made of.

## The arbitration, and seventeen years of litigation

From the 自由時報 report of the final judgment[^ltn-2005] — the
best-documented part of the whole affair and the part least often told
accurately:

| Stage | Outcome | |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Matra's claim | 「廿多億元」 — over NT$2 billion, for time added by others' delays | [^ltn-2005] |
| Negotiation | Collapsed | [^ltn-2005] |
| Arbitration, 6 October 1993 | For Matra: awards in NT dollars, US dollars and francs totalling ≈ **NT$1.025 billion**, plus 5% annual interest | [^ltn-2005] |
| DORTS sues to annul the award | Loses; the Supreme Court upholds the award in November 2000 | [^ltn-2005] |
| DORTS resists enforcement | Argues the claim is time-barred: a works contract carries a short limitation period, expired since 1998. **Two courts agree.** | [^ltn-2005] |
| Supreme Court remands | The High Court, rehearing the case, reclassifies the contract as 「買賣、承攬的混合契約」 — a mixed sale-and-works contract — carrying a **fifteen-year** limitation. Matra is in time. | [^ltn-2005] |
| Supreme Court, 22 July 2005 | DORTS's appeal dismissed. Principal plus twelve years' interest ≈ **NT$1.64 billion.** | [^ltn-2005] |

Sit with the pivot for a moment, because it is the best irony in Taipei's
transit history. The city very nearly won: two courts accepted that Matra had
simply waited too long. What defeated it was the *character of the contract* —
the rehearing found that a deal covering route definition, structural design,
and the purchase, installation and testing of vehicles and equipment was not
mere contracting but a purchase of a system, with a purchase's fifteen-year
limitation[^ltn-2005]. **Taipei lost the lawsuit for the same reason it had
lost control of its railway: it had bought a turnkey system.** The clause that
cost it NT$1.64 billion[^ltn-2005] and the lock-in that cost it its maintainer
were the same decision, read twice.

*The judgment itself has not been read here — only the reporting of it. The
full sequence between 2000 and 2005 involves stages this page has deliberately
left undated, because the accounts found disagree on the intermediate
chronology and the judgment text would settle it. See the foot of the page.*

## The withdrawal, and the year nobody can pin down

**That Matra pulled its people out is not in dispute. When, exactly, is.**

〈木柵線〉 says it happened 「在通車兩個月之後」 — two months after the March
1996 opening, so around May 1996[^zh-muzha]. The same encyclopedia's article
on the through line says it happened in **1997**, after a contract dispute,
when Matra 「將技術人員全數撤離，不再提供維修服務」[^zh-wenhu]. Both readings
circulate; this site could not separate them, and no contemporaneous news
report of the walkout itself has been found. One reconciliation — staff
leaving through 1996, maintenance formally ending in 1997 — is plausible and
is exactly the kind of guess this site does not publish as fact. **No year is
asserted here.**

What the period looked like from inside city hall has, since March 2026, an
account from the mayor himself: 陳水扁 describes a fight over spare-parts
pricing — Matra quoting NT$10,000 for a patented rubbish bin is his example —
and the company using the threat of pulling its technicians as leverage in the
renegotiation[^mirror-2026]. A memoir published on the line's thirtieth
anniversary is a primary source with an interest in its own story, and it is
cited here as that; but note that it frames the withdrawal not as a sudden
exit but as a **negotiating instrument that was eventually used**.

商業周刊 was writing about the relationship under the title
「恩怨情仇」 — grudges and feuds — by 30 May 1996, six weeks after
opening[^bw-1996]. That article is the closest thing to a contemporaneous
account of the collapse; it is paywalled, has not been read past its opening
here, and is cited only for what its date and title establish: whatever the
formal end date, the marriage was publicly over within weeks of the trains
carrying passengers[^bw-1996].

## Keeping a metro nobody would maintain

What Taipei was left holding was a driverless system whose builder would not
service it — proprietary control software, proprietary parts, and no
second source for either.

黃令名's account — and this is his research speaking, singular and
attributed, not a settled record — is that Taiwanese engineers kept the line
running by 「偷偷複製法方半成品的列車控制程式、請台廠製造甚至赴光華商場取得
電子零件」: covertly copying the French side's half-finished train control
software, commissioning parts from Taiwanese manufacturers, and sourcing
electronics at Guanghua Market, Taipei's consumer electronics
bazaar[^huang-2021]. He calls this 「技術能動性」 — technological agency — and
it is fair to say his article exists to make that argument[^huang-2021].

陳水扁's retelling corroborates the substance from the client's side, minus
the market stalls: replacement components were developed with 中研院, 中科院,
工研院 and private industry until every part had a domestic alternative,
cheaper than the originals[^mirror-2026]. Between a scholar's account of
improvisation and a politician's account of mobilisation sits what actually
happened; both agree the parts stopped coming from France and the trains kept
running.

For the cost of the other road, 黃令名 points at Jacksonville, Florida — a
city that faced a comparable dead end with its own Matra system and resolved
it by demolition, rebuilding with a Bombardier monorail[^huang-2021]. Taipei's
choice to keep the French railway alive without the French is the road that
led, eventually, to this site's [Wenhu Line](/rail/lines/wenhu-line/) page
describing one seamless operation.

## The political ledger

> 「馬特拉不拉，我們自己拉」[^ltn-2008][^mirror-2026]
>
> 陳水扁, then Mayor of Taipei — *if Matra won't pull, we'll pull it ourselves*

The dispute consumed three mayors, one per act.

- **黃大洲** presided over the fires. 自由時報's commentary holds that the
  木柵線 debacle 「間接導致了當時台北市長黃大洲的敗選下台」 — indirectly cost
  him the 1994 election[^ltn-2005]. A newspaper asserting why an election was
  lost is a genre with a poor record; the timing fits, the claim is widely
  repeated, and it is published here as the paper's judgment rather than a
  fact.
- **陳水扁** opened the line on 28 March 1996[^zh-muzha] and gave the affair
  the epitaph above. 自由時報 canonises it as his 名言[^ltn-2008]; his own
  account of saying it, thirty years later, has it blurted out mid-crisis in
  the parts-pricing fight[^mirror-2026]. The line is quoted everywhere and
  dated almost nowhere — no contemporaneous record of the moment of utterance
  has been found, and its setting rests on the speaker's memory.
- **馬英九** was mayor when the final bill arrived in July 2005, and said the
  city would pay what the judgment required[^ltn-2005]. The DORTS director,
  常岐德, declined all interviews and switched off his phone[^ltn-2005]. The
  commentary's arithmetic was blunter: 「全民買單繳『學費』，足足要花十六億
  四千萬元」 — the public pays the tuition, all NT$1.64 billion of
  it[^ltn-2005].

## What it changed: buying the Neihu extension

When the line's northern half came to be tendered, DORTS changed the two
things that had produced the trap, on 黃令名's analysis of the
procurement[^huang-2021]:

1. **CBTC was specified** — communications-based train control, an open
   signalling approach — rather than an extension of Matra's proprietary
   fixed-block system[^huang-2021].
2. **Civil works and E&M were let together**, as one integrated contract,
   rather than split across contractors whose mutual delays had just spent
   seventeen years in court[^huang-2021].

By then the incumbent was not even Matra. Siemens had taken control of Matra
Transport International in the 1990s — 95% by October
1998[^lagardere-siemens] — and completed the takeover in November 2001,
renaming the business Siemens Transportation Systems[^metro-mag-matra]. VAL
was now a Siemens product, and a tender written around VAL would have had
exactly one credible bidder. 黃令名's argument is that the CBTC-plus-turnkey
structure existed precisely so that Siemens could not name its price as the
only party able to extend the existing system[^huang-2021]. Procurement
rationales are the kind of thing institutions tidy up afterwards, and his is
the only account found[^huang-2021] — but it is consistent with what was
actually bought.

What was bought was Bombardier. And here the engineering gets genuinely
elegant, because Bombardier could not supply a VAL — VAL belonged to a
competitor. So it built the [Innovia APM 256](/rail/rolling-stock/innovia-apm-256-c370/):
a people mover dimensioned to a French railway it had no rights to, matching
the running-surface spacing, the side guide bars and the 750 V supply taken
off them, with a car body 20 mm narrower than the
[VAL256](/rail/rolling-stock/val256/) it would run beside[^zh-innovia]. The
incumbent's guideway became the tender's interface specification.

The last act was the cutover. Rather than run two signalling systems, the
whole line switched to Bombardier's CITYFLO 650 on the day the extension
opened — **4 July 2009** — and the 1996 Matra fleet was withdrawn that same
day for conversion, returning on 26 December 2010 after eighteen months with
its French control system replaced[^zh-val256][^zh-wenhu]. 黃令名 adds — his
account alone; nothing else found says it — that CITYFLO had been tested at
night on the Muzha Line's own tracks while the Matra system carried
passengers by day[^huang-2021]. It is a striking detail, it is single-source,
and those two properties are related; it is recorded here with its
attribution showing.

The last Matra-controlled train ran thirteen years after Matra stopped
maintaining any of them. Nobody from the company was there to see it, and by
then there was no company: the name had been gone from the rail business for
eight years[^metro-mag-matra].

## What is not established

The gaps, recorded so they are searched for rather than papered over:

- **The contract itself.** No tender documents, competing bids or evaluation
  records were found; they would sit in DORTS's construction archive.
- **The judgments.** Taiwan's court decisions are indexed and searchable, and
  the 22 July 2005 Supreme Court civil judgment ought to be retrievable; it
  would move the whole litigation section from reportage to primary record,
  and settle the intermediate dates this page declines to give. The
  single highest-value follow-up here.
- **A contemporaneous report of the withdrawal**, which would close the
  1996-versus-1997 question above.
- **Any accident investigation** into the 1993 fires, which would confirm or
  kill the coupling explanation two secondary sources agree on.
- **Matra's side.** Every substantive source on this page is Taiwanese. The
  French trade press of the day, Lagardère's archive and Siemens's own history
  of the VAL business would give the other account, and none has been found.
  Its absence is the largest structural bias in this page, and it is not a
  small one.
