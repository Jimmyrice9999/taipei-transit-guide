## A trunk line completed in 1908, on a gauge already a decade old

The west-coast trunk line (縱貫線) that became the core of today's TRA network
was completed on 20 April 1908, when construction proceeding from both the
north and south met in central Taiwan.[^zhwiki-railway-history] A formal
opening ceremony, the 縱貫鐵道全通式, was held over three days from 22 to 24
October 1908 at Taichung Park, presided over by Imperial prince
閑院宮載仁親王; the 湖心亭 pavilion still standing in the park today was built
as the ceremony's commemorative structure.[^twmemory-trunk-ceremony] The
chief engineer through the project was 長谷川謹介 (Hasegawa Kinsuke), invited
to Taiwan in 1899 by civil administrator 後藤新平 and appointed technical
head of the 臨時臺灣鐵道敷設部; he remained until the line's completion and is
popularly credited as "臺灣鐵道之父" (father of Taiwan's railways).[^gjtaiwan-hasegawa]

A widely repeated but unverified claim credits the Japanese administration
with choosing Taiwan's 1,067 mm ("cape") gauge. That is not what the record
actually supports. The
gauge was fixed years earlier, under Qing governor 劉銘傳's 1887–1893
Keelung–Hsinchu line, and the Japanese administration continued it rather
than choosing it — a continuation that carried a direct military and
logistics benefit, since 1,067 mm was also the gauge of Japan's own
home-islands railways (fixed in 1872), meaning Japanese rolling stock could
run directly onto existing Taiwan track without conversion.[^twreporter-gauge]
No source found in this research names the individual or the specific
reasoning behind the original 1887 gauge choice beyond the general
cost-saving logic of a narrower gauge in mountainous terrain, and no source
found documents any proposal to widen the trunk line to a broader gauge
during Japanese rule — both remain open questions.

The separate 臺東線 (East Coast Line) used a narrower gauge still, 762 mm,
reflecting the lower traffic the Railway Bureau anticipated for the less
developed east coast at the time it was built; it was only later widened to
1,067 mm to connect with the trunk network.[^zhwiki-railway-history] The
alternate low-gradient 海線 (coastal line) between Zhunan and Changhua, built
to bypass the original mountain route's steep grades, opened in
1922.[^zhwiki-zongguan-line]

## Stations that survive, and stations that don't

Four Japanese-era trunk-line stations still stand as heritage buildings.
新竹車站's current (fourth-generation) building was completed 31 March 1913,
designed by 松崎萬長 (Matsuzaki Manchō), an architect trained in Germany, in a
style blending German Gothic elements with Baroque detailing; it was
designated a national monument (國定古蹟) on 1 July 1999.[^zhwiki-hsinchu-station]
臺中車站's old building, "臺中驛," was completed 31 March 1917 in a
late-Renaissance brick style following the same "辰野式" idiom used on the
former Governor-General's Office; it was designated a protected monument in
1995 and the designation's scope was expanded in August 2021 to cover a
second platform and an early parking-office building — the station itself
stopped regular operation on 15 October 2016 and is now preserved in place as
a plaza landmark.[^zhwiki-taichung-station] 臺南車站's current building was
completed in March 1936, designed by 宇敷赳夫 of the Railway Bureau's own
improvement section; it was bombed by US forces in 1945, subsequently
repaired, and remains in service today, designated a national monument on 18
December 1998.[^zhwiki-tainan-station]

Two structures did not survive. 基隆車站's third-generation building, an
English-influenced Renaissance-style brick station with a clock tower,
opened 30 October 1908; TRA itself demolished it in 1965–1967, judging it too
small for rising traffic, and opened a fourth-generation reinforced-concrete
replacement in January 1967.[^zhwiki-keelung-station] The 臺灣鐵道飯店 (Taiwan
Railway Hotel), a red-brick building near what is now Taipei Station,
completed in October 1908 and designed by a team of five architects
including Matsuzaki Manchō, was destroyed in the Taipei Air Raid of 31 May
1945; a postwar annex briefly reopened in 1946 before being reassigned to
other government use and eventually demolished, and the site is now occupied
by commercial towers.[^zhwiki-railway-hotel]

## A ten-week gap, then a new administration

Japan announced its surrender on 15 August 1945, but Republic of China forces
did not arrive to take over civil administration until 25 October 1945 — a
gap of roughly ten weeks during which the Governor-General's administrative
structure, including the Railway Bureau, continued to operate.[^zhwiki-railway-history]
On 1 November 1945 the new Taiwan Provincial Administrative Executive
Office's Communications Department established a Railway Management
Committee (鐵路管理委員會), which took over the Railway Bureau and the
network's private-company lines, organised into departments for general
affairs, operations, mechanical, engineering, materials, accounting, motor
vehicles and regional offices in Taipei, Kaohsiung and
Hualien.[^zhwiki-railway-history] On 1 March 1948 that committee was formally
reorganised into the Taiwan Railway Administration (臺灣鐵路管理局) under the
provincial government — the direct institutional ancestor of today's
TRA.[^zhwiki-railway-history] A government archive describing its own
holdings confirms that surviving Railway Bureau-era records run from Meiji
33 (1900) to Shōwa 20 (1945), a range consistent with the handover this page
describes, though the archive-holdings page itself does not narrate the
handover.[^archives-railway-holdings]

## Records still missing

No source reached in this research names who specifically chose the original
1887 gauge or documents a wartime debate over changing it. The full identity
and title of the outgoing Japanese railway director at the 1945 handover, and
the precise scale of wartime damage to rolling stock and bridges, were not
traced to a source solid enough to publish here — a search-engine-derived
figure of 1,392 destroyed rolling-stock units was not confirmed against a
fetched primary or secondary page and is recorded as TBC rather than stated
as fact. An academic paper on Hasegawa Kinsuke's specific technical
decisions (蔡龍保, 國史館學術集刊 6, 2005) was located but remains unverified,
since it could not be extracted from its PDF in this pass. Detailed
segment-by-segment opening dates between 1899 and 1908 are not established
here either — they exist only behind Wikipedia footnote markers this
research did not individually trace to their underlying primary documents.
