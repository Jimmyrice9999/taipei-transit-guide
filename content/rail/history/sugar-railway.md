## A network that peaked well past 3,000 km

Taiwan's sugar railway network traces to April 1908, when the first
sugar-company line opened; a year later, on 20 May 1909, Yanshui Harbor
Sugar Co. opened Taiwan's first officially licensed public passenger line,
between Xinying and Yanshui.[^archives-sugar-history] By the end of Japanese
rule the network totalled 2,964.6 km, and continued to grow after the 1945
handover to reach 3,244 km by 1952 — a figure roughly double the length of
Taiwan's conventional railway network at the same date.[^archives-sugar-history]
A separate encyclopedia account, not independently verified against a
primary source, states the passenger-carrying portion of the network peaked
in 1953–1954 at 41 lines totalling approximately 675
km.[^zhwiki-sugar-railway]

## Passenger service, 1909 to 1982

Passenger service declined through the 1960s under road and bus competition
and ended entirely in August 1982, when the Beigang Sugar Factory's Chiayi
line made its final run — closing out 74 years of continuous commercial
passenger operation on the network.[^archives-sugar-history][^taisugar-history]
Both the government archive and the operator's own history page agree on the
month and year; neither gives the exact day, so the specific date within
August 1982 remains unconfirmed.[^archives-sugar-history][^taisugar-history]
In 1979 the network's remaining coal-fired steam locomotives were withdrawn
as operations converted to diesel.[^taisugar-history]

## What survives today

Only the Huwei Sugar Factory still operates a cane-hauling freight line,
16.1 km long, to bring raw cane to the mill — the sole survivor of a network
that once moved sugarcane on more than 3,000 km of track.[^taisugar-history]
The operator's own material gives this line's name as 馬光厝線, but
Wikipedia's dedicated article on what appears to be the same line spells it
馬公厝線 and gives a shorter figure, 12.9 km, for what it describes as the
line's core segment rather than the whole
system.[^taisugar-monthly-mahguangcuo][^zhwiki-mahguangcuo-line] Both the
naming and the length are published here as a conflict rather than resolved:
the operator's own pages, fetched independently twice, consistently use 馬光厝,
while Wikipedia's dedicated article uses 馬公厝 — this could reflect a genuine
place-name variant, a historical respelling, or an error on one side, and no
source found addresses the discrepancy directly.

Five former sugar-factory sites now run short tourist trains rather than
freight. At 蒜頭 (Suantou) in Chiayi's Liujiao Township, the operator
extended its tourist line to reach the Chiayi High Speed Rail station in
2022 and, per a news report quoting an on-record MOTC Railway Bureau
official, extended it again to the Palace Museum Southern Branch on 7
October 2023, adding roughly 0.4 km of new
track.[^taisugar-attractions-suantou][^ltn-suantou-extension] A separate,
unverified search-engine figure putting the whole HSR-and-museum extension
package at 4.6 km conflicts with the 0.4 km museum-spur figure and is not
used here, since it was never traced to a fetched page.[^ltn-suantou-extension]
烏樹林 (Wushulin) in Tainan's Houbi District began its tourist-train era in
2001, when a graduate student's proposal to Taiwan Sugar led the operator to
reopen the line to visitors — the operator's own page frames 2001 as the
start of a "new chapter," which may or may not be the same milestone as an
unverified secondary claim that scheduled service specifically began in
November 2011, a discrepancy this page does not resolve. 溪湖 (Xihu) in
Changhua activated a boarding facility in 2003, and 橋頭 (Qiaotou) in
Kaohsiung, Taiwan's first modern mechanised sugar factory and a
municipally designated cultural landscape, runs a tourist line connecting
the factory grounds to the Kaohsiung Flower Horticultural Center — neither
of the operator's own pages for these two sites gives a current route length.

## Records still missing

The exact day within August 1982 that passenger service ended is not
established by the sources checked here. The correct spelling and full
length of the Huwei survivor line are not established either — the
operator's own material and Wikipedia disagree on both. Current route
lengths for the Xihu and Qiaotou tourist lines are unverified, as is the
exact date 烏樹林's tourist service began, since a 2001 proposal date and a
possible later scheduled-service launch date have not been reconciled. A Yunlin County
place-name gazetteer or a Taiwan Sugar internal asset register would settle
the Huwei naming question; the operator's own route maps or timetables, not
located in this pass, would settle the two tourist-line lengths.
