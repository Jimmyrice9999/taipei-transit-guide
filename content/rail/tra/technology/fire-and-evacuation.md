## A proposed standard, and no confirmation it was adopted

A 2005 report commissioned by the predecessor of today's Railway Bureau
concluded that Taiwan's existing building and fire codes were hard to apply
to railway tunnels and underground stations, and proposed standards for a
future regulation — but the report states plainly that it is reference
material for a future standard, not an adopted one.[^rb-2005-tunnel-fire-report]
Taiwan's general fire-safety-equipment regulation, read directly, confirms
the gap the report describes: it contains no provision naming railway
tunnels or underground railway stations as a distinct
category.[^law-moj-fire-equipment-standard] This page could not confirm
whether a tunnel-specific standard has since been formally adopted; MOTC
does publish separate standards for elevated railway stations and for
highway tunnels, neither of which covers this subject.

The 2005 report's own proposed figures — 80 cm safety walkways on both
sides, staggered hydrant boxes every 50 m, marked emergency phones every 45
m, and cross-passages no more than 244 m apart, citing the US standard NFPA
130 — are not confirmed as adopted or as installed in any named TRA
tunnel.[^rb-2005-tunnel-fire-report] Both North Link Line's Xinguanyin
Tunnel (opened 2003) and the Sanyi–Houlong new alignment's Sanyi Tunnel
(opened 1998) predate this report, and no source found states these figures
were built into either.

## What a real accident's own investigation found, and didn't reach

The Taiwan Transportation Safety Board's investigation into the 2 April 2021
Qingshui Tunnel derailment, on the North Link Line, found that the train's
conductor did not fully understand the safety-equipment design and use
affecting passenger survival — including emergency ventilation and
lighting — and that TRA's own conductor manual did not include technical
information on the quantity, function and location of train safety
equipment; it recommended TRA strengthen emergency-response equipment,
crew documentation and training.[^ttsb-qingshui-report] These findings
concern on-board train equipment and crew knowledge, not the tunnel's own
fixed infrastructure — this page found no TTSB finding specifically
addressing the tunnel's fixed evacuation facilities in the material it
could access, and the full investigation report was not obtainable beyond
the TTSB's own summary page.[^ttsb-qingshui-report]

## A different kind of tunnel, under Taipei

TRA operates three underground emergency stopping stations in the Taipei
underground section — Ximen, Fuxing and Guangfu — not used for regular
passenger or freight service. Ximen's is reported as equipped with
ventilation, lighting and an emergency escape passage direct to ground
level, on a 300 m single-side platform, with a stated evacuation target of
guiding passengers to ground level within 5 minutes.[^ltn-emergency-stations]
Ximen has been shared with Taiwan High Speed Rail as an emergency facility
since 5 January 2007.[^zhwiki-ximen-emergency-station] These are urban
underground stations, not long mountain tunnels, and this page does not
treat them as governed by the same standard discussed above — no source
found connects the two.
