"The first thirty minutes are free if you are transferring from the metro"
is the version of YouBike most visitors hear, and it describes one six-year
window in the system's life rather than the whole of it — and not even the
current window[^cna-2024][^youbike-free30]. The fuller story, drawn from the
city's own press releases and Taiwanese reporting, is a public bicycle
system that has run three different fare policies and two mutually
incompatible generations of hardware, in place, without ever closing.

## A BOT scheme that started with eleven stations

Taipei's public bicycle system began as a build–operate–transfer contract
let to Giant under then-mayor Hao Lung-pin, piloted in Xinyi District from 11
March 2009[^ntbike-milestones][^zhwiki-youbike]. Citywide operation started
on 30 August 2012, and by the time that initial trial period ended on 30
November 2012 the system had already passed one million cumulative
rides[^ntbike-milestones].

## Free, then not free, then free for some, then free again

This is the part almost nobody outside Taiwan gets right, because most
accounts freeze on whichever policy was current when they were written.

**The system launched with the first 30 minutes free, and stayed that way
until 1 April 2015**[^newslens-2015][^storm-2015]. Mayor Ko Wen-je's
administration then introduced a flat NT$5 charge for that first half-hour,
arguing the free period was subsidising recreational riding rather than
commuting[^storm-2015]. New Taipei, whose own network was still under
construction, pointedly did not follow — it kept its first 30 minutes free
through the rest of 2015, budgeting roughly NT$70 million a year to do
so[^newslens-2015]. What the 2015 change actually saved Taipei's budget is
itself disputed: one account puts the monthly subsidy fall at NT$20 million
to NT$8 million, another at NT$20 million to NT$13
million[^storm-2015][^newslens-2015] — the same policy, two different
numbers, neither resolved here.

**A narrower discount followed in 2018**: NT$5 off when transferring to or
from a bus or the MRT, rather than a free period for everyone[^cna-2024].
This is the version of the story that made it into English coverage, and as
a snapshot of 2018–2024 it isn't wrong — it is just not the whole
arc, and it stopped being current on **28 February 2024**, when Mayor Chiang
Wan-an restored an unconditional first-30-minutes-free policy for
registered members citywide and the transfer discount was cancelled the same
day, since a universal free period made a transfer-specific one
redundant[^youbike-free30][^cna-2024]. The city projected a 15% ridership
increase and about 7,000 tonnes a year of avoided carbon
emissions[^einfo-2024]. Ride a YouBike 2.0 in Taipei today and, if you are a
registered member, that first half hour costs nothing regardless of where
you are coming from; beyond it, the standard rate runs NT$10 per 30 minutes
for up to four hours, NT$20 to eight hours, and NT$40
beyond[^youbike-free30].

## Why there is a 2.0 at all

The generational split is a genuinely engineering-driven story, and the
city's own documents say so plainly. YouBike 1.0's docks needed a municipal
power connection: the station-siting standard the transport department
published on 23 March 2015 required, alongside a roughly 600-metre spacing
principle and a minimum 2-metre clear sidewalk width, that a station sit
near an existing power supply[^dot-siting-2015]. That single requirement —
road excavation, a utility connection, a minimum viable scale to justify
it — was what capped how fast and how densely the network could
grow[^dot-20-plan].

YouBike 2.0's answer was to move the intelligence off the dock and onto the
bicycle. In the transport department's own words, the redesign
「免除市電需求，控制器移轉車機」 — removes the need for mains power and
shifts the controller onto the bike itself[^dot-20-plan] — which let
station siting respond to terrain and sidewalk width instead of utility
access[^dot-20-plan]. The physical dock changed too: 1.0 parked two bikes
per post, 2.0 parks one[^dot-20-launch]. The trade was a lighter, cheaper,
more flexible dock for total incompatibility with the existing fleet — a
1.0 card or bike could not use a 2.0 station, or vice versa, from the day
the trial began.

That trial started at 10:00 on 15 January 2020, around Gongguan MRT Station
and National Taiwan University: 102 stations sharing about 1,800 docks, and
500 new bicycles, according to the launch announcement[^dot-20-trial]. A
follow-up release one month later, describing what reads as the same pilot
area, instead gives 600 new bicycles[^dot-20-usage] — a discrepancy between
two official releases six weeks apart that neither states an explanation
for and that this page does not attempt to resolve. Full service across
Taipei began at 10:20 on 8 May 2021: 160 new stations that day on top of the
existing 103 in Gongguan and around NTU, for 263 total, with a year-end
target of 750 stations and new batches of stations activated every Tuesday
and Friday[^dot-20-launch]. New Taipei's own 2.0 network reached full
service on 20 October 2021[^ntbike-milestones].

## Retiring 1.0

By October 2022, YouBike 2.0 had already reached its 1,200-station citywide
target, and 1.0 — the system that had carried Taipei's bike-share for
thirteen years — accounted for just 8.5% of rides, turning over 1.8 times a
day per bike[^dot-10-retire]. Taipei switched it off entirely at 00:00 on 3
December 2022, after more than 200 million cumulative
rides[^dot-10-retire]. New Taipei kept its own 1.0 fleet running for a
while longer, and the mismatch produced a real inconvenience at the
boundary: a rider who picked up an orange 1.0 bike in New Taipei and carried
it into Taipei after the cutover faced a NT$350 fee to have it collected,
because there was nowhere in the city left to return it to[^businesstoday-2022].

Retired 1.0 bicycles went three ways. The city transferred 1,106 to its own
agencies, for neighbourhood patrols and school cycling-safety education, and
donated 1,300 to Indigenous communities (原鄉)[^dot-10-memorial], marking
the retirement with a memorial installation at MRT Taipei City Hall Station
Exit 3 — the system's very first station[^dot-10-memorial]. Bicycles judged
beyond further use were sold to a recycling contractor in late 2021 at
NT$41 each; the city's own clarification states this reflects the cost of
disassembly, transport, labour and disposal rather than an underpriced sale
of public property[^dot-10-scrap]. That NT$41 is a scrap value, not a
replacement cost, and this page does not have a sourced figure for what an
original YouBike 1.0 actually cost the city to buy — a NT$10,000 figure has
circulated but was not found in any source this run, and Chinese Wikipedia's
NT$13,000 carries no citation of its own[^zhwiki-youbike].

## Electric assist, and where things stand

YouBike 2.0E — the electric-assist version — launched simultaneously in
Taipei and New Taipei on 30 August 2024, giving a stated 80 to 100 km of
range on a full charge on flat ground, with no free period or subsidy: a
flat NT$20 per 30 minutes for the first two hours, NT$40 beyond, on the same
docks and app as standard 2.0[^youbike-20e-launch]. It does not get the
first-30-minutes-free benefit or the TPASS 基北北桃1200 discount that
standard 2.0 carries in Taipei, with a carve-out only for holders of
Taipei-issued senior or disability transit cards[^dot-20e-faq] — a
distinction easy to miss if a source treats "YouBike" as one undifferentiated
product.

As of a Taipei transport department release dated 14 August 2025, the city
was operating 1,633 stations and 23,860 bikes, including 2,000 e-assist
models, and was closing in on 400 million cumulative Taipei
rides[^dot-400m]. That milestone came fast: 100 million to 200 million took
about three and a half years, 200 million to 300 million took two and a
half, and 300 million to 400 million took seventeen months — entirely inside
the window opened by the February 2024 fare change[^dot-400m]. Across every
