Taiwan's taxi law separates a traditional 小黃 (yellow taxi) from an
app-dispatched 多元化計程車 (diversified taxi) by dispatch method, not by
technology. The Motor Vehicle Transportation Industry Management Rules
(汽車運輸業管理規則) define a taxi business simply as renting a small
passenger car to carry passengers within an approved area: 「計程車客運業：在核定區域內，以小客車出租載客為營業者。」[^motor-vehicle-rules]
An amendment dated 25 October 2016 added the diversified-taxi category on
top of that base definition, describing it as an app-based service that
matches supply and demand for booking-only dispatch.[^motor-vehicle-rules][^taipei-legal-database-history]

## What actually distinguishes the two categories

The rule is dispatch method, stated directly in Article 91: a diversified
taxi may only accept pre-booked rides through the platform, and may not
street-cruise for passengers or wait at a taxi rank — 「不得巡迴攬客或於計程車招呼站排班候客」.[^motor-vehicle-rules]
Colour follows the same split. A standard taxi's replacement or new vehicle
must be painted the pure yellow of Taiwan Paint Manufacturers' Association
colour card no. 1-18, and the same article explicitly bars a diversified
taxi from using that colour: 「但多元化計程車不得使用前開車身顏色」.[^motor-vehicle-rules]
A 2019 amendment, Article 103-1, extended the licensing logic to rental-car
businesses partnering with information platforms: their drivers must hold
taxi-driver qualifications, and vehicles cannot be booked for less than an
hour or hailed on the street.[^taipei-legal-database-history]

## The Uber fight, and how it actually ended

Uber's Taiwan service launched officially on 31 July 2013.[^uber-timeline-bnext]
Regulators treated it as an unlicensed taxi operation, and by early 2017 had
issued dozens of citations; a single 2 February 2017 order alone carried a
NT$231 million fine.[^uber-timeline-bnext] The Highway Act's penalty
provision for unlicensed operation sets a fine range of NT$100,000 to
NT$25,000,000 per violation, a range fixed by a January 2017
amendment.[^highway-act] Uber suspended and restarted its Taiwan service
more than once through this period, eventually shifting to a
rental-car-partnership model that Article 103-1 was written to
formalize.[^uber-timeline-bnext][^taipei-legal-database-history] Uber itself
stated in October 2019 that it would not form its own diversified-taxi
fleet, instead acting as a platform for existing licensed 多元計程車
operators.[^uber-timeline-bnext]

The dispute's actual legal resolution is narrower than "Uber was ruled
legal." On 18 September 2020, the Supreme Administrative Court's Grand
Chamber ruled on case 109年度大字第2號, brought by 交通部公路總局 (the central
Highway Bureau) against 台灣宇博數位服務股份有限公司 (Taiwan Uber Digital
Services Co., Ltd.).[^judicial-yuan-uber-ruling] The court's own account is
a jurisdictional holding, not a finding that Uber's operating model was
lawful: the central Highway Bureau has no authority to fine or order the
closure of an unlicensed taxi operator whose head office sits in a special
municipality (直轄市) — that power belongs to the municipal government, not
the national regulator.[^judicial-yuan-uber-ruling] Reported totals for how
much of Uber's accumulated fines this unwound vary sharply across secondary
sources, from a single NT$20 million/215-notice docket tied to this specific
case up to a NT$1.77 billion/196-case figure covering everything the Highway
Bureau reportedly withdrew afterward; this page does not resolve which
figure is correct, since no source explains how the two totals relate.
