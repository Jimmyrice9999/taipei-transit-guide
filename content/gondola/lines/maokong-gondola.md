Taipei's cable car climbs from the zoo to a tea-growing hillside on a line
that is, for a tourist ride, unusually well documented by the government
that owns it — because in 2008 one of its towers nearly fell into a
residential neighbourhood, and the Control Yuan wrote a 22-page corrective
resolution about why[^cy-corrective].

## The system

The Maokong Gondola opened 4 July 2007, built by Taipei City's New
Construction Office to a design supplied by the French ropeway manufacturer
POMA, with 財團法人中華顧問工程司 (renamed 台灣世曦工程顧問股份有限公司 partway
through the project) and 武林建築師事務所 supervising and 春原營造股份有限公司
building it as design-build contractor under a 最有利標 ("most advantageous
tender") award of 2 May 2005[^twce-t16][^cy-corrective]. It was Taipei's
first cable car[^gov-taipei-40m] and is reported as Taiwan's longest gondola
route[^english-gondola].

The line runs 4.033 km — published as 4.03 km throughout the construction-era
and Control Yuan record, almost certainly the same figure carried to one more
decimal place on the operator's current site rather than a real
disagreement[^gondola-system]. It has **four passenger stations** — 動物園站
Zoo Station, 動物園南站 Zoo South Station, 指南宮站 Zhinan Temple Station, and
貓空站 Maokong Station — plus **two non-boarding angle stations** the
alignment needs because it is not straight ("略呈7字型," roughly
7-shaped)[^gondola-system][^english-gondola]. Zoo South Station opened under
the name 動物園內站 ("Zoo Interior Station") and was not renamed to its current
form until 26 October 2015[^zhwiki-zoo-south] — which is why the Control
Yuan's 2009 report on the same station still calls it 園內站. One-way
journey time is given by the operator as **20 to 30 minutes**[^gov-taipei-40m];
zh.wikipedia separately gives approximately 17 minutes, sourced to an
in-article footnote not independently checked here[^zhwiki-gondola] — both
figures are published rather than one chosen over the other.

The system's own current page counts **25 pier bases carrying 47 tower
columns**, implying most bases carry a two-legged frame; the Control Yuan's
2009 resolution instead gives a single figure of 25 towers for the whole
line[^gondola-system][^cy-corrective]. The whole project's cost is given two
ways in that same Control Yuan document — an approximate **NT$1.3 billion**
in its opening summary and a precise **NT$1,108,870,438** later in the text
discussing the planning contractor's survey work — without the document
explaining whether the two figures cover different scopes[^cy-corrective].

## The T16 failure

In September 2008 three typhoons crossed Taiwan in eighteen days — 辛樂克
(11–16 September), 哈格比 (21–23 September) and 薔蜜 Jangmi (26–30
September)[^cy-corrective]. Their combined rainfall reached **1,271.5 mm**
for the month in the Muzha area — the Central Weather Bureau's second-highest
September figure there in 22 years[^cy-corrective]. Jangmi's own contribution on 28 September alone was
separately measured at roughly 500 mm over Wenshan District, and it was that
single day's rain that triggered the report the city acted
on[^twce-t16].

The slope below tower **T16** began failing on 27 September; its first two
sections slid on 28 September, sending debris into the 政大御花園社區 community
below and prompting the evacuation of roughly 72 households (about 200
people) that night, with the debris stopping about 20 m short of the nearest
building[^cy-corrective][^twce-t16]. A third, residual section of the slope
came down after further heavy rain on 9 November. The city suspended
service at **15:00 on 1 October 2008**[^cy-corrective][^twce-t16].

Taipei's Department of Industrial Development commissioned **four Taipei
professional associations** — civil, geotechnical, structural, and soil-and-
water-conservation engineering — to assess the failure, T16's remaining
safety, and whether the tower should be reinforced in place or moved. Their
initial report came 30 November 2008, the full report 12
December[^cy-corrective][^twce-t16]. It found T16's well-type foundation
(井式基礎) left cantilevered by roughly 4 metres, with the overturning moment
the exposed foundation would face in an earthquake exceeding what its
existing reinforcement could carry — though continuous tilt monitoring
installed from late September 2008 found no measurable tilt, displacement or
settlement in T16 or any other tower over the following three
months[^cy-corrective]. In-place reinforcement was judged technically
possible if carried out early in the slide, which the city did not do; by
the time it acted, conditions had worsened enough that the assessors
recommended relocation as the safer course[^cy-corrective].

**Taipei City Government relocated T16**, rather than rebuilding it in
place — a decision the Control Yuan's own corrective resolution explicitly
endorses. Engineers compared two candidate drill sites (NH-1, NH-2) toward
neighbouring tower T17; POMA's own engineers preferred NH-1 as closest to
the original tower's centreline and elevation, and both candidate sites
tested with better geology than the original. Mayor Hau Lung-bin announced
the new site at a press conference on 23 January 2009; **the rebuilt tower
stands about 30 m from where the old one stood**[^cy-corrective].

The Control Yuan's own November 2009 investigation — carried out by
committee members 杜善良, 葛永光 and 陳永祥 over more than eleven
months[^cy-case-summary] — went well beyond T16 itself. It rejected public
allegations that the foundation piling had been built with substandard
materials or that the site had no bedrock at all, but separately found fault
with all three of the project's engineering contractors — the planning firm
鼎漢公司, project manager 台灣世曦, and design-build contractor 春原營造 — for
inadequate geotechnical survey work along the whole alignment, and with the
city government itself for a slow initial emergency response and for never
requiring a proper site-wide geological survey in the original planning
contract[^cy-corrective].

## Reopening

The gondola reopened in stages in March 2010: Mayor Hau announced on 18
March that formal service would resume 30 March; TRTC published the detailed
reopening rules on 21 March; a six-day **free trial** ran 23–28 March, 10,000
free rides a day, 102,000+ riders over the six days; and **formal, paid
service resumed 30 March 2010**[^zhwiki-gondola]. Two figures both appear in
sources for "the reopening date" — 23 March and 30 March — and neither is
wrong: they are the free trial's start and the formal reopening,
respectively, both individually dated in the same source[^zhwiki-gondola].

The reopening had two small early scares. On 7 April 2010 a tilt-monitoring
alarm on neighbouring tower **T15** turned out to be a false alarm, with no
actual movement found[^zhwiki-gondola]. On 9 April, one week after reopening
and with cumulative ridership past 100,000, a cable-detection alarm halted
the system for 4 minutes 5 seconds that evening — no passengers were
trapped, and service resumed within the hour[^zhwiki-gondola]. By 29 April,
ridership since reopening had reached 270,000[^zhwiki-gondola].

Over its first operating period — 4 July 2007 to 1 October 2008, about 15
months — the gondola carried more than **6.17 million riders**, per
zh.wikipedia's own footnoted figure[^zhwiki-gondola].

## The crystal cabins

From the 30 March 2010 reopening, one pilot cabin ran with a transparent
floor[^gondola-crystal]. The full **"貓纜之眼" ("Eyes of Maokong") crystal-cabin fleet — 31
units — entered service from 2 November 2010**[^gondola-crystal]. Each
floor is three layers of tempered glass, about 48 mm thick and 213 kg,
CE-certified, and the extra weight costs three seats: a crystal cabin
carries 5 passengers against a standard cabin's 8[^gondola-crystal][^gondola-fares].
zh.wikipedia separately gives the crystal cabins' individual numbers as
1–15, 66 and 133–147 (car 66 the original prototype) and puts the whole
fleet, standard and crystal together, at **147 cabins including 3
spares**[^zhwiki-gondola] — a figure not independently checked against a
primary fleet roster, though the 31-cabin crystal count matches the
operator's own page.

## Fares and rules

A single one-way ride costs NT$180 full fare / NT$50 discount
fare[^gondola-fares]; an unlimited all-day pass is NT$300 /
NT$100[^gondola-fares]. Upgrading to a crystal cabin adds NT$50 per person
per trip; chartering a whole cabin adds NT$600 per trip[^gondola-fares].
Groups of 20 or more get 10% off an all-day pass, 40 or more get 20%
off[^gondola-fares]. Discount fares are for Taipei City residents, retired
city civil servants and teachers, children 6–11, seniors 65 and over (or
Taipei-registered Indigenous residents 55 and over), and people with
disabilities plus one companion — shown at boarding and tapped through a
dedicated gate with a stored-value card[^gondola-fares]. EasyCard and other
stored-value cards work across the whole system[^gondola-fares]. The
operator's current fare page makes no mention of the TPASS 基北北桃1200 monthly
pass as an accepted option; a 2023 council-session report says TRTC had
evaluated adding the
gondola to TPASS but chose not to, to avoid complicating ticketing during the
pass's early rollout[^gondola-fares][^ettoday-tpass].

Service suspends automatically for a sustained gust of 16 m/s (roughly
Beaufort 7) for three seconds or more, any gust of 18 m/s (Beaufort 8) or
more, lightning on the route, an earthquake of intensity 4 or greater, or
when a land typhoon warning covers Taipei and either weather or a city
work/school closure calls for it[^gondola-suspend]. Routine maintenance
closes the system on scattered Mondays through the year, plus a separate,
longer annual-overhaul block of consecutive Mondays in some months — the
published 2026 calendar does not follow a simple "first Monday of the month"
pattern[^gondola-hours].

## Now

The gondola is operated by **North Metro Recreation Company (北捷遊憩事業股份
有限公司)**, a 2025 joint subsidiary of Taipei City Government and TRTC that
also runs Taipei Arena and the Taipei Children's Amusement
Park[^tmr-profile]. Cumulative ridership passed **40 million riders on 19
May 2026**, ahead of the system's 20th operating anniversary in July
2027[^gov-taipei-40m].
